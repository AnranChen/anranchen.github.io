The recent ESMO 2025 and AACR-NCI-EORTC Triple Meeting showcased a flood of data in the RAS pathway space (see table). While first-generation KRAS G12C inhibitors such as sotorasib and adagrasib are already approved and commercialized, next-wave G12C programs continue to demonstrate meaningful activity in post-G12C settings with manageable safety profiles.

Meanwhile, KRAS G12D agents are rapidly advancing, including both small-molecule inhibitors and degraders. Notably, Astellas' ASP3082 remains the only KRAS G12D degrader with clinical readouts to date, with ASP4396 and Arvinas' ARV-806 recently entering the clinic.

Yet, the most disruptive story in RAS oncology may not be mutation-specific at all.

Daraxonrasib, a pan-RAS(ON) inhibitor targeting KRAS, NRAS, and HRAS, continues to lead the field with compelling Phase 2 data and ongoing Phase 3 programs. With an FDA breakthrough therapy designation in PDAC, the asset could see potential approval as early as 2026 or 2027—pending successful confirmation in 2L PDAC. Pan-KRAS compounds have also entered clinical trials in 2025.

These data naturally raise the question: Does the momentum behind pan-KRAS/RAS inhibitors diminish the strategic value of G12C and G12D programs?

## 1. Why Pan-RAS/KRAS Is Surging

The strategic rationale is compelling for pan-RAS/KRAS approaches. KRAS mutations are present in ~25% of all cancers, concentrated in three key indications:

- **NSCLC** — G12C > G12V ≈ G12D
- **PDAC** — G12D > G12V > G12R
- **CRC** — G12D > G12V ≈ G13D

Even in these dominant settings, G12C and G12D each represent less than half of KRAS-mutant patients. A single pan-KRAS/RAS agent could deliver broad coverage across multiple tumor types, simplify biomarker logistics, streamline commercial execution, and reduce portfolio fragmentation.

Biology also supports this approach. Targeting KRAS or all RAS proteins — both mutant and wild-type — can potentially limit compensatory feedback through wild-type signaling. Notably, Revolution Medicines is pursuing combination strategies pairing mutation-selective inhibitors (elironrasib, zoldonrasib) with daraxonrasib — an emerging multi-layered RAS blockade model.

## 2. The Risks of Pan-RAS: Toxicity & Timing

Toxicity remains a key concern. Targeting wild-type RAS can cause side effects. Pan-RAS(ON) inhibitor daraxonrasib reported grade ≥3 TRAEs of 35% in PDAC and 16% in NSCLC. By contrast, G12C/G12D agents at ESMO and Triple 2025 generally reported a lower rate of grade ≥3 TRAEs.

For KRAS, it is unclear whether pan-KRAS inhibitors will show higher toxicity, as consolidated clinical data are still lacking—but the uncertainty itself is a risk.

The timing gap also matters. G12C inhibitors had a clear head start with approvals in 2021. G12D programs entered first-in-human (FIH) trials in late 2023 to early 2024, while most pan-KRAS agents are 1–2 years behind—except the pan-RAS(ON) agent daraxonrasib (FIH 2022), which is technically not a pan-KRAS inhibitor. The regulatory finish line will be key. A 1–2-year lag remains within the normal variability of oncology development timelines, depending on data quality, regulatory engagement, and strategic prioritization.

## 3. Why G12C & G12D Still Matter

Despite the rise of pan-RAS/KRAS approaches, G12C and G12D programs remain highly relevant for several reasons:

First, **the head start advantage**. G12C already holds regulatory approvals and continues to expand its clinical footprint, while G12D assets are progressing toward pivotal potential.

Second, **favorable risk-benefit profiles**. They offer lower toxicity risk and clearer commercialization routes, thanks to known cleaner safety profiles, established regulatory pathways, and established biomarker frameworks. For investors and partners, these are mature, de-risked stories with strong deal-making momentum.

Third, **combination potential**. As the field moves toward multi-layered RAS blockade strategies, mutation-selective inhibitors remain valuable combination partners alongside pan-RAS/KRAS agents.

## 4. Pan-RAS/KRAS Is a Game Changer, But the Game Is Not Over Yet

What to watch next? 2026 will be pivotal: we should expect RP2D safety and early efficacy signals from pan-KRAS programs, alongside pivotal data from G12D and next-generation G12C assets. Pan-RAS inhibitor daraxonrasib could see a first approval as early as late-2026 to 2027.

Combination studies are accelerating: G12C/G12D are advancing into first-line and adjuvant trials; pan-RAS (daraxonrasib) is the near-term breadth backbone for salvage and combo strategies; pan-KRAS is early, likely starting in salvage before any move earlier. Deal activity is expected to intensify, spanning licensing, co-development, and geographic rights, as both mutant-selective and pan-RAS approaches continue to shape the next phase of RAS-targeted oncology.

The question is not whether G12C and G12D inhibitors still matter—they do. The question is how they will coexist with, complement, or compete against pan-RAS/KRAS strategies in an increasingly crowded and sophisticated landscape.
