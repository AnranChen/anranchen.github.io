When the RASolute 302 data finished on the plenary screen, the room stood. It is worth pausing on that before any discussion of mechanism, sequencing, or competitive position, because a standing ovation at ASCO is not given for a hazard ratio. It is given when a disease that has outlasted the field for decades finally gives ground.

Metastatic pancreatic cancer is about as hard as oncology gets. Roughly half of patients are diagnosed after the disease has spread, five-year survival in the metastatic setting sits near 3%, and second-line chemotherapy has offered only six to seven months of median survival. For patients and the clinicians who treat them, this is the first time a RAS-directed therapy has changed the trajectory of this disease in a randomized phase 3.

For chemical biology, the meaning is just as large. KRAS was the canonical "undruggable" target for forty years. The achievement should be named as such before anything else.

## 1. What the Plenary Showed

While the topline had been public since April, the plenary's job was to show the texture beneath the headline. The intent-to-treat and the RAS G12 co-primary populations told nearly the same story: OS 13.2 vs 6.6–6.7 months (HR 0.40), PFS 7.2–7.3 vs 3.5–3.6 months (HR 0.45–0.49), confirmed response 31.6–33.2% vs 11.2–11.8%. The efficacy is not carried by a single endpoint or a single analysis population.

The genuine surprise was tolerability. A drug that hits wild-type RAS was widely expected to pay for breadth in toxicity, and against chemotherapy it did the opposite: fewer grade ≥3 treatment-related events (43.6% vs 57.5%), far fewer discontinuations (1.2% vs 11.2%), and fewer dose reductions (36.1% vs 57.5%). The discontinuation gap deserves a caveat — chemotherapy toxicity forces patients off treatment while responders on an oral agent stay on, so some of that spread is structural. But the dose-reduction comparison does not have that confound: it is a within-treatment event, and daraxonrasib patients were on drug far longer (median 6.2 vs 1.5–3.2 months), with more time to accumulate toxicity, and still required fewer reductions. What reductions they did need were driven by on-target, manageable effects — rash, stomatitis, diarrhea, largely grade 1–2 — rather than the myelosuppression and neuropathy that define chemotherapy.

One honest limitation belongs here too. At the February data cut, median follow-up was only about 8.5 months while median OS reads 13.2 months, with an upper confidence bound not yet estimable. The direction and magnitude of benefit are clear, but the precise survival tail will firm up only with longer follow-up. This is a strong result still maturing.

## 2. "Regardless of RAS Mutation Status" — A Closer Look

One of the most quoted lines recently about daraxonrasib is that it works "regardless of RAS mutation status". That framing is true in the sense the trial was designed to show, but a close reading of the recent NEJM publication reveals more nuance.

RAS G12 mutations account for roughly 80–85% of metastatic PDAC in the real world; RASolute 302 enrolled an even higher share, about 92%. The whole-population benefit is, therefore, substantially a G12 benefit wearing a pan-RAS label — and within G12 it is overwhelmingly G12D/G12V (OS HR 0.38). Step outside G12D/V and the evidence thins quickly. The "other G12" subgroup crosses 1.0 on both survival and progression. And in the non-G12 subgroup — G13, Q61, or no RAS mutation identified, only about twenty patients per arm — overall survival is nominally favorable (HR 0.37) while progression-free survival actually favors chemotherapy (HR 1.38, with most progression events on daraxonrasib), both with wide 95% CIs. The "regardless of mutation" story is best read as a reasonable bet supported mainly in G12D/V, not an established fact across RAS — consistent with where the FDA placed the Breakthrough and Orphan designations: G12-mutant, previously treated disease. The benefit outside G12D/V remains inconclusive.

For all the debate it draws, the G12-only-versus-all-comers label question is a small one commercially. It swings perhaps 15–20% of the second-line population, a minor uncertainty next to the bigger questions.

## 3. Where the Platform's Value Actually Sits

The bigger commercial question that actually decides the platform's value is not 2L mPDAC. Second-line pancreatic cancer is a meaningful achievement on its own, but it is not where the weight of the opportunity sits. Other settings matter more:

**1L PDAC.** The treated first-line pool is roughly two to two-and-a-half times the second-line pool, with longer treatment duration earlier in disease, and RASolute 303 is the trial that addresses it (daraxonrasib alone, daraxonrasib plus gemcitabine/nab-paclitaxel, or gemcitabine/nab-paclitaxel alone; co-primary PFS and OS). RASolute 302 has de-risked the biology and the safety assumptions, but 303 carries risks 302 did not: a first-line comparator far tougher than second-line chemotherapy, harder recruitment into a more competitive landscape, and a three-arm design that dilutes statistical power. The early first-line signals are encouraging (combination response ~55–58%, monotherapy ~47%), but they are early.

**Lung and colon.** A pan-RAS mechanism logically reaches KRAS-mutant NSCLC and RAS-mutant colorectal cancer, both larger than PDAC and both in trials, and the bars are higher in each. NSCLC is G12C's home, with established selective agents and IO-combination strategies, so a broad agent has to justify why more pathway inhibition beats a clean mutant-selective drug. Colorectal is harder still: EGFR-mediated feedback blunts RAS monotherapy, and the pancreatic effect size should not be assumed to transfer. The pancreatic result is the proof of concept, not a forecast for the other two.

## 4. How the Readout Reshapes the RAS Landscape

Last year I [discussed the pan-RAS and pan-KRAS programs against mutation-selective G12C and G12D agents]({% post_url 2025-11-02-kras-g12cd-pan-ras %}). RASolute 302 begins to answer the toxicity question that hung over the pan-RAS approach, and in doing so shifts the bar each class is measured against. The pancreatic readout does not land evenly across the RAS landscape. It pressures mutant-selective, pan-KRAS, and pan-RAS programs to different degrees, and it is worth reading those effects separately.

**Mutation-selective inhibitors** keep their core biological argument: a G12C- or G12D-selective drug spares H- and N-RAS and wild-type KRAS, and that selectivity should translate into a narrower, lower-grade toxicity profile than an agent that inhibits the active state across all of RAS. Daraxonrasib's registrational grade ≥3 treatment-related rate of 43.6% — favorable against chemotherapy — is the relevant anchor here, and the cross-comparison with the early profiles of selective agents such as zoldonrasib and ASP3082 (setidegrasib) is exactly the trade-off this implies. The changing factor is that the benchmark for the selective agents has moved. For G12D in particular, the comparator is no longer chemotherapy; it is daraxonrasib. A G12D-selective drug now has to demonstrate a survival readout in the same neighborhood and win on tolerability, which is a narrower path than competing against chemo. G12C, meanwhile, still contends with the first-generation incumbents in NSCLC — and daraxonrasib, not yet read out in lung cancer, will soon press there too.

**Pan-KRAS** relies on a Goldilocks position: a larger addressable population than any single mutant-selective drug, with less wild-type-RAS exposure than full pan-RAS. But daraxonrasib's better-than-feared safety has compressed precisely the advantage pan-KRAS was meant to monetize — the room to be "broad but gentler" is thinner than it looked. The needle is still threadable; the eye is smaller.

**Combinations** are where the threads most plausibly converge. A switch-II-pocket binder is mechanistically orthogonal to a tri-complex, which gives pan-KRAS and mutant-selective SII-P binders a coherent rationale to pair with a pan-RAS(ON) backbone rather than simply compete with it. On the other hand, Revolution Medicines — with its own selective tri-complex toolkit alongside daraxonrasib — has the operational advantage of building those combinations in-house, without the friction a cross-company pairing would require.

**Pan-RAS** is the toughest game for any fast-follower. Every credible competitor shares the same tri-complex, cyclophilin-A-mediated design, so the first-in-class advantage is large and durable. Absent a demonstrated edge in DMPK or potency, a second pan-RAS(ON) agent will struggle to differentiate on concept alone.

## 5. The End of the Hard Science, the Start of the Hard Strategy

It is possible to hold two things at once. RASolute 302 is a landmark — the first targeted therapy to bend the survival curve in metastatic pancreatic cancer, and the clinical validation of an approach the field spent decades believing was impossible. It earned its ovation. What it did not do is settle anything downstream: whether the benefit extends cleanly beyond G12D/V, whether first line converts, whether a pan-RAS mechanism travels to lung and colon, and how mutant-selective, pan-KRAS, and pan-RAS strategies divide the work. The ovation marked the end of the hard part of the science. It marked the beginning of the hard part of the strategy — for RevMed and its competitors alike.
